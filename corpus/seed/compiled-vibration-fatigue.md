# Vibration fatigue in bolted joints (compiled)

## Mechanism

A bolted joint fails under vibration by one of two distinct mechanisms, and
confusing them leads to the wrong fix.

*Self-loosening* is a loss of preload: transverse slip at the interface lets the
thread helix rotate, and clamp load falls until the joint is free. It is a
kinematic problem, not a strength problem.

*Fatigue fracture* is crack growth in the bolt itself, driven by cyclic tensile
stress. Counter-intuitively, higher preload usually reduces fatigue risk,
because a well-clamped joint transmits load through the interface rather than
cycling the bolt.

## Diagnosis from evidence

- Fasteners found loose but undamaged, no fretting: self-loosening.
- Fretting powder or polished witness marks at the interface: transverse slip,
  the precursor to self-loosening.
- Bolt fractured at the first engaged thread with a flat beach-marked face:
  fatigue, from insufficient preload or an under-sized bolt.
- Bolt fractured with a cup-and-cone face: overload, not fatigue. Look for a
  shock event, not a vibration source.

## Remedies ranked by effectiveness

1. Increase preload toward 75 percent of proof load, if the joint can carry it.
   This addresses both mechanisms at once and costs nothing per unit.
2. Eliminate transverse slip: increase friction, add a dowel or shoulder, or
   reduce the joint's compliance.
3. Use a prevailing-torque or wedge-ramp locking feature. These slow
   self-loosening; they do not restore lost clamp load.
4. Serrated flange or spring washers. Weakest of the four, and a spring washer
   under a class 10.9 bolt embeds and further reduces preload.

Thread-locking adhesive belongs at rank 3 in effect but requires clean, dry
threads and cure time, which field procedures rarely allow.
